## 1. Samhain

### What the evidence supports

- **The name and season.** Samhain (Old Irish *samain*) is usually glossed as "summer's end". It
  marked the start of winter in the Gaelic year, when cattle came down from the summer pastures
  and herds were culled before winter [S1].
- **Early Irish literature.** Samhain appears in the earliest Irish literature, from about the
  9th century. The 10th-century tale *Tochmarc Emire* lists it first of four seasonal festivals,
  and *Serglige Con Culainn* describes a week of gatherings, feasting and contests around it
  [S3 via S4, pp. 361–363].
- **The otherworld.** Britannica's entry describes Samhain as a time when the world of the gods
  was believed to draw near and become visible to people, and when offerings were needed to get
  through that dangerous season [S2, excerpt]. Ronald Hutton notes that many supernatural tales
  are set at Samhain but suggests this may be because it was when royal and warrior assemblies
  met, much as Arthurian tales are set at Christmas or Pentecost [S3 via S4, pp. 362–363].
- **Christianisation.** All Saints (1 November) and All Souls (2 November) came to occupy the same
  days. Whether the 1 November date was chosen to replace Samhain is disputed: Frazer argued for a
  Celtic origin, Hutton for a Germanic one [S3 via S4].

### Where popular claims overreach

- **"The Celtic New Year."** This idea goes back to the philologist John Rhŷs and was popularised
  by James Frazer. Hutton calls the evidence for it flimsy and finds no medieval record of a
  pan-Celtic festival on 1 November [S3 via S4, pp. 362–363]. Many modern Pagans still treat
  Samhain as the ritual start of their year [S1], and educational resources often state it as
  fact [S5]. The game says "many Pagans mark it as the turn of the year", not "the ancient Celtic
  New Year".
- **"Druids" and "human sacrifice".** Popular articles describe druids performing rites and
  sacrifices at Samhain [S17]. The sources are thin and mostly hostile Roman or later Christian
  writing. The game makes no such claims.
- **"Trick-or-treat comes from Pagan rites."** A Pagan Federation education officer writes that
  there is no evidence that customs like trick-or-treat came from Pagan rites for warding off or
  inviting back the dead, and that modern Pagans more likely adapted a Christian festival than the
  reverse [S1].

### Modern Pagan and Wiccan observance

Samhain is one of the eight festivals of the Wheel of the Year, kept by many but not all modern
Pagans, usually on 31 October or on 1–2 November [S1]. It is a time to honour ancestors: family
members, and ancestors of place or tradition [S1]. Wiccans count it among the greater sabbats and
often treat it as the most important [S6].

**The thin veil.** Many Pagans say the veil between the living and the dead is thinnest at Samhain
[S5] [S14] [S15]. This is both a folk belief with deep roots (the otherworld drawing near [S2]) and
a modern framing in its present wording. The game uses the phrase in Nana's voice and in the
notebook, always as "what Nana believes" or "what many Pagans say", never as narrator fact.

**What the game uses:** Samhain as Nana's festival of remembrance, the start of her year, and the
night the veil is thin; the notebook page separates Gaelic history, modern practice and the
game's own invention. **[needs practitioner review]**: how Nana describes Samhain in her own words.

## 2. The Wheel of the Year

Eight festivals, roughly six weeks apart (northern-hemisphere dates; groups vary) [S5] [S6]:

| Festival | Date (approx.) | Theme |
|---|---|---|
| Samhain | 31 Oct – 1 Nov | Summer's end; the dead and the ancestors |
| Yule | winter solstice, c. 21 Dec | Rebirth of the sun; lights and evergreens |
| Imbolc | 1–2 Feb | First signs of spring; associated with Brighid |
| Ostara (spring equinox) | c. 21 Mar | New life |
| Beltane | 30 Apr – 1 May | Start of summer; fertility |
| Litha (summer solstice) | c. 21 Jun | Longest day |
| Lughnasadh (Lammas) | 31 Jul – 1 Aug | First harvest |
| Mabon (autumn equinox) | c. 21–22 Sep | Second harvest; the turn into the dark half |

The four "fire festivals" (Samhain, Imbolc, Beltane, Lughnasadh) are Gaelic; the solstices and
equinoxes are astronomical. The eight-fold calendar as a set is modern: it does not seem to be
traceable before the 1950s and was put together by Gerald Gardner and Ross Nichols [S1] [S5]. The
name "Mabon" for the autumn equinox has no historical pagan equivalent [S6].

**What the game uses:** one notebook page with the wheel drawn as a circle and Samhain at the top.
It says plainly that the wheel is a mid-20th-century arrangement of older festivals.

## 3. Wicca as a living religion

### Origins

Wicca came to public notice through Gerald Gardner, a retired British civil servant, with
*Witchcraft Today* (1954); Doreen Valiente was among those who shaped its rites in the 1950s [S6].
Hutton's history of the movement concludes that, whatever Gardner claimed, no surviving Wiccan
tradition can be traced before about 1900, and that its beliefs drew on scholars, poets and
novelists who had revived the figures of Pan and the Goddess [S7, from reviews]. The Covenant of
the Goddess describes Wicca as the most popular expression of Neo-Paganism, reviving pre-Christian
European practice and adapting it to modern life [S8].

### Belief and practice (it varies)

- **Nature-based.** Worship centres on nature and the turning seasons [S9].
- **Goddess and God.** Many Wiccans honour a Goddess (often as Maiden, Mother and Crone) and a
  Horned God; others are Goddess-only (Dianic), polytheist, pantheist, or treat the deities as
  archetypes [S6]. The game never pins Nana to one theology.
- **Covens and solitaries.** Wicca has no central authority or creed and is organised in local
  covens [S9]. CoG is itself "a confederation of covens and solitaires of various traditions"
  [S8]. Many Wiccans practise alone, some with self-initiation [S6]. Nana belongs to a small coven
  but keeps her household Samhain alone with Wren.
- **The Book of Shadows** is a working book of rituals, spells, poems and chants, not scripture;
  Gardner meant it to be copied and adapted [S6].
- **The pentacle** (a five-pointed star in a circle) is Wicca's most common symbol; its points are
  read as earth, air, fire, water and spirit [S6].

### The Wiccan Rede

The usual short form is "An it harm none, do what ye will" [S6]. CoG's code of ethics opens with
the wording "An ye harm none, do as ye will" [S8]. It is read as freedom to act joined to
responsibility: you answer for what you do, and you avoid harm [S6]. The couplet was made public by
Doreen Valiente in the 1960s, after Gardner's death, building on Gardner's phrasing [S6]. It is not
Crowley's "Do what thou wilt" and should not be presented as permission to do anything.

**What the game uses:** "harm none" is the rule Nana lives by; she says it once, plainly, when
Wren asks why the offering is an apple and not anything more dramatic. **[needs practitioner review]**

### Legal recognition in the US

- **Dettmer v. Landon**, 799 F.2d 929 (4th Cir. 1986): the court affirmed that Wicca is a religion
  for First Amendment purposes, though it upheld the prison's refusal of particular ritual items
  [S10, excerpt] [S9]. Popular sources sometimes overstate this as "official recognition"; the
  holding is narrower.
- **The 2007 pentacle settlement.** On 23 April 2007 the Department of Veterans Affairs settled
  lawsuits brought by Americans United and the ACLU and added the pentacle to its approved emblems
  of belief for government headstones and markers [S11] [S9].
- Military chaplains' guidance recognises Wicca, and New York recognised Wiccan clergy for
  marriages in 1985 [S9].

**What the game uses:** one notebook line: Wicca is a recognised religion in the US, and since
2007 the pentacle can be carved on veterans' headstones. Nana's late husband could be a veteran
whose stone carries one (optional scene). **[needs practitioner review]**

### Misconception: Wicca is not Satanism

Wiccans do not worship Satan, a figure from Christian theology. The Covenant of the Goddess and
the Pagan Federation spent years correcting the false idea that Wiccans were Satanists, a claim
that was dangerous during the "Satanic panic" of the 1980s and early 1990s [S13, excerpt]. The
1974 statement of the Council of American Witches is widely reported to say Witches do not worship
"Satan" or "the Devil" as Christianity defines them; this document has not yet been read from a
primary copy, so the game does not quote it.

**What the game uses:** no devil imagery anywhere near Nana, the coven or the circle. If a
neighbour voices the misconception, the scene corrects it gently and the neighbour is not a villain.

## 4. Ancestor practices at Samhain

- **Ancestor altars.** Some Pagans keep an altar of photographs, belongings, candles and incense,
  and attend to it especially at Samhain [S1]. A Pagan writer describes altars with photos of the
  dead, candles, fallen leaves and seasonal fruit [S14].
- **The silent supper ("dumb supper").** Some hold a silent meal with an empty place set for the
  dead, sometimes eaten in reverse order, dessert first [S1]. Covens and circles leave an empty
  place for spirits and ancestors at their feasts [S14].
- **Offerings.** At one public rite, the names of those who died in the year are read and offerings
  such as bread, honey and mead are given to a fire [S1].
- **Candles in windows.** In Irish folk custom, candles were lit in the windows on All Souls'
  Night to light the souls of the dead on their way, and people kept indoors that night. The
  National Folklore Collection's Schools' Collection (1937–39) records this from Co. Cork among
  other places [S12, excerpt]. This is a Catholic folk custom, not a Wiccan one; some Celtic
  Reconstructionist Pagans keep a similar candle in a west-facing window [S4]. The game says where
  the custom comes from and that Nana has taken it into her own practice.
- **Pets.** At a Southern Illinois Pagan Alliance Samhain, the ancestral altar carried pictures and
  keepsakes of departed family, friends and pets [S15]. This is one community's practice, reported
  by a student newspaper; it supports Nana remembering Smoke but is not evidence of a general rule.

**What the game uses:** Nana's altar (photos, Smoke's collar, a candle); the four family
lanterns; the empty chair at Nana's table. The search for Smoke's spirit, and Smoke being
*seen*, is the game's fantasy. **[needs practitioner review]**: Smoke on the ancestor altar, and
whether "lighting the dead home" is something Nana would say.

## 5. Kitchen witchery, herbs, apples and cider

- **Rosemary for remembrance.** Ophelia: "There's rosemary, that's for remembrance. / Pray you,
  love, remember." (*Hamlet* 4.5; Folger through-line numbers 3098–3099) [S16]. Rosemary was
  long associated with remembering the dead, so the line suits a remembrance brew.
- **Apples at Halloween.** Apple games were Halloween courtship divination: apples tagged with
  names and bobbed for, or hung on strings and snapped at, to foretell a sweetheart [S17]. In
  Ireland, a peel thrown over the shoulder was said to form a future spouse's initial [S18 via S4].
  Lisa Morton, quoted in [S17], doubts the popular claim that the Romans brought Pomona's apples to
  Samhain, but notes that apples ripen in October and would have been plentiful. Apple-bobbing is
  still associated with Samhain in modern Pagan teaching [S5].
- **Cider.** No source located ties cider specifically to historical Samhain. Offerings of drink
  (mead) appear in modern Pagan Samhain rites [S1]. Cider in the game is a New England harvest
  drink and Nana's own choice, and it is presented that way.
- **Simmer pots and "kitchen witchery"** are modern popular practice; no scholarly source was
  located. The game shows a pot of apple, rosemary and cinnamon, stirred with intention, and
  claims nothing more for it. **[needs practitioner review]**

**What the game uses:** the cider recipe (apple, rosemary, cinnamon), the apple offering, and an
optional apple-bobbing tub at the Whitfields' party (a Halloween game, not a rite).

## 6. Halloween customs

- **Guising.** In Scotland, children dress up and visit houses as guisers, and must perform, with
  a joke or a song, to get a reward; treats were once nuts and apples and are now sweets [S20].
  Guising is first recorded in 16th-century Scotland and later in Ireland, Mann and Wales [S4].
  A 1929 Scottish case distinguished guising from begging [S20].
- **Turnip lanterns.** In 19th- and early 20th-century Ireland and Britain people carved turnips
  (and potatoes or beets), lit with candles [S19]. The National Museum of Ireland holds a plaster
  cast of a turnip lantern from Fintown, Co. Donegal, of a kind made there around 1900; turnips
  were carved to frighten people on 31 October, and the pumpkin is an American development of the
  custom [S21].
- **Stingy Jack.** The name "Jack o' Lantern" comes from a folktale of Jack, turned away from both
  Heaven and Hell and left to wander with only a lantern [S21] [S19].
- **Trick-or-treating in the US.** Folklorist Tad Tuleja dates it to the 1930s; Thanksgiving
  masking was a direct forerunner, and even 1940s articles on Halloween rarely mention it [S22].
  It spread in the 1920s–30s in Irish and Scottish immigrant communities alongside a wave of
  pranking [S26, excerpt], and became general after the war. Jack Santino's essay for the Library
  of Congress is the standard short history [S23, not read].
- **Porch lights and hours.** Many New England towns set official trick-or-treat hours, often 5–7
  or 5–8 pm (e.g. Hamilton, MA: 5–8 pm [S27]), and ask households taking part to leave the porch
  light on [S28, excerpt]. Halloween 2026 is a Saturday.

**What the game uses:** jokes for treats (guising), porch light on = knock here, trick-or-treating
from dusk until 9 pm (a little late but plausible on a Saturday), a carved turnip on Nana's step
beside the pumpkins, and Stingy Jack as a story a neighbour tells.

## 7. Black cats, familiars and shapeshifting

- **Familiars** in English witch trials were supernatural spirits said to serve the accused, often
  in animal form: Agnes Waterhouse's cat "Satan" (1566), ferret-shaped spirits at St Osyth (1582),
  mice, dogs and others; Matthew Hopkins cited names like Pyewacket and Vinegar Tom as proof [S24].
  Cats were only one of many forms. Familiars appear in these records as agents of harm, in
  "confessions" taken by questioners from people who were then tried and often executed [S24].
- **Shapeshifting** turns up in the same literature: Waterhouse's cat was said to become a toad and
  a dog [S24]. Witches who change into hares or cats are a wider European folklore motif.
- **This is not Wiccan doctrine.** It is persecution literature and folklore about witches, written
  by accusers. Modern Wiccans have pets, and some use "familiar" affectionately, but no source here
  makes transformation or spirit-servants part of the religion.
- **Black cats and welfare.** There is no evidence that people deliberately harm black cats at
  Halloween; many shelters once paused black-cat adoptions in October, and some now run Halloween
  adoption promotions instead [S25].

**What the game uses:** Wren's cat form is labelled in the notebook as "a fairy-tale gift for one
night, borrowed from old stories about witches, not something Wiccans believe or do." No witch-trial
names, no "Satan" the cat. Smoke is Nana's ordinary, beloved pet. An optional end-credits line can
mention black-cat adoption.

## Sensitivity notes

These rules govern the game's writing and art. Where they conflict with a design idea, the rule
wins.

**Do**

1. Show Wicca as a living religion practised by ordinary people. Nana is a warm, funny neighbour
   who gardens, bakes and votes; her faith is one part of her life, not a costume or a plot twist.
2. Keep magic in the register of real practice: candles, herbs, intention, words said aloud,
   remembering names. "Spells" are small and kind. **[needs practitioner review]**
3. Use the pentacle only respectfully: on Nana's altar cloth, a pendant, perhaps a headstone. It is
   never a pickup, an enemy marker, a jump-scare or decoration on spooky set dressing.
4. Keep the costume witch visibly a costume: green face paint, plastic hat, a broom from the hall
   closet. Let Nana enjoy it; the game can have her say it is a fine costume and not what she does.
5. Label fantasy as fantasy. The cat transformation and visible spirits get an in-game note that
   they are the game's fairy tale, not Wiccan belief.
6. Say where each custom comes from: window candles are an Irish All Souls custom Nana adopted
   [S12]; guising is Scottish and Irish [S20]; the Wheel of the Year is mid-20th-century [S5].
7. Let grief be gentle. The four family lanterns are for real-feeling losses; treat each family's dead
   with care, and give no lantern a comic payoff.
8. Write neighbours of different faiths and none, including a churchgoer who is kind to Nana.

**Don't**

1. No blood, sacrifice, skulls-on-altars, inverted crosses, goats' heads, devils or demons anywhere
   connected with Nana, her coven or the circle.
2. No curses, hexes, love spells or revenge magic from Nana, and no "evil witch" antagonist.
3. Don't present contested history as fact: no "ancient Celtic New Year", no "druids sacrificed on
   Samhain", no unbroken line from the Celts to Wicca [S3] [S7].
4. Don't put real coven rites, oaths or Book of Shadows text in the game; initiatory material is
   private to traditions that keep it secret [S8]. Nana's words are original to the game.
5. Don't borrow Día de los Muertos imagery (ofrendas, calaveras, marigold arches, papel picado) or
   any closed or Indigenous practice (e.g. smudging with white sage) for Nana's Samhain.
6. Don't joke about witch-trial victims or use their names; no Salem gags, no gallows props.
7. Don't show the coven as sinister: no hooded figures in silhouette, no chanting stingers. If the
   coven appears, it is a few friends with a flask of tea.
8. Don't show harm to cats or imply black cats are unlucky or evil.
9. Don't make the "Satanism" misconception the joke; if voiced, it is corrected kindly.

**Items marked for practitioner review**

- §1: Nana's own description of Samhain, the thin veil, and the turn of the year.
- §3: Nana's statement of the Rede, and the optional veteran's-pentacle scene.
- §4: Smoke on the ancestor altar; "lighting the dead home" in Nana's voice; the empty chair.
- §5: the cider simmer pot and the words Nana says while stirring.
- The midnight offering at the hilltop circle (apple, cider, candle): what Nana says, whether
  she casts a circle, and whether she would do this alone with a child or with her coven.
- Sensitivity "Do" 2: what counts as a "spell" in the game.
- Every notebook page about Wicca, Samhain and the Wheel of the Year.
